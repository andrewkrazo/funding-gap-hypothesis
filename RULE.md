## 1. The rule being tested

> **The Funding-Gap Rule:** *A building boom ends in a bust when builders' cash flow
> falls short of their spending and lenders step back.*

**Where it comes from.** Ray Dalio has argued that bubbles usually burst when holders
have to sell assets to raise cash. Forbes reports him saying the bubble "usually pricks
at that" moment of selling wealth to get money. Source: Fiona Riley, "Billionaire Ray
Dalio Warns AI Bubble Could Soon Burst," *Forbes*, Oct 7, 2026, reporting his remarks at
the Forbes Global CEO Conference in Singapore
(<https://www.forbes.com/sites/fionariley/2026/10/07/billionaire-ray-dalio-warns-ai-bubble-could-soon-burst/>).
This is a news report of spoken remarks, not Dalio's own written text. Applying the idea
to **builders** (firms spending heavily on long-lived assets) and to the **cash-flow gap
plus lender pullback** is **my own extension, not Dalio's claim.**

**Why test it this way.** In "Principled Thinking and AI Need to Go Together"
(Ray Dalio, *Principled Perspectives* Substack, June 10, 2026,
<https://raydalio.substack.com/p/principled-thinking-and-ai-need-to>), Dalio describes
writing a principle down from cause-and-effect logic, back-testing it against history,
and not deriving principles by data mining. This project follows that order: state the
logic, fix the definitions, then test.

**The causal story, in plain words.** A firm that spends more on building than its
business brings in has to cover the gap with outside money (loans, bonds, new shares).
If lenders pull back at the same time, the firm can't fill the gap. It then has to cut
spending hard, sell assets, or default. Either condition alone should be survivable:
a gap with willing lenders gets funded, and a lender pullback doesn't matter much to a
firm that funds itself.

## 2. Definitions (plain words, then code-ready)

All quantities are per firm, per **calendar quarter** `q`.

### 2.1 Quarterly cash-flow values from SEC filings

- **Source:** SEC EDGAR XBRL `companyfacts` API,
  `https://data.sec.gov/api/xbrl/companyfacts/CIK##########.json`.
  Every value we use keeps its accession number (`accn`), form, filing date and a URL.
- **Year-to-date problem.** Cash-flow statements in 10-Qs are year-to-date. Quarterly
  values are computed by differencing within a fiscal year:
  `Q1 = YTD(Q1)`, `Q2 = YTD(H1) − YTD(Q1)`, `Q3 = YTD(9M) − YTD(H1)`,
  `Q4 = FY (from the 10-K) − YTD(9M)`. Two YTD facts are only differenced if they share
  the same fiscal-year start date. A fact whose duration is already one quarter
  (80–100 days) is used directly.
- **Restatements.** Primary: **as first reported.** For each (start date, end date)
  period, use the fact from the earliest-filed document, since that is what lenders and
  investors saw at the time. Sensitivity: latest-filed values. Both get reported.
- **Calendar quarter mapping.** A fiscal period is assigned to the calendar quarter that
  contains its end date. Exception: an end date in the first 7 days of a calendar quarter
  (for 52/53-week fiscal years, e.g. Jan 3) goes to the previous quarter.
- **No filling.** A missing quarter stays missing. Nothing is interpolated, estimated or
  carried forward. Any trailing-four-quarter (TTM) value that needs a missing quarter is
  missing.

### 2.2 Operating cash flow (OCF)

Cash generated by the business itself.
Tag priority: `NetCashProvidedByUsedInOperatingActivities`, then
`NetCashProvidedByUsedInOperatingActivitiesContinuingOperations`.

### 2.3 Capital expenditure (capex)

Cash paid to build or buy long-lived productive assets. **Includes** buying oil and gas
acreage, because land is part of a building boom (like a homebuilder buying lots).
**Excludes** buying whole companies (`PaymentsToAcquireBusinessesNetOfCashAcquired`),
since that is a takeover, not building.

Candidate tags:
`PaymentsToAcquirePropertyPlantAndEquipment`, `PaymentsToAcquireProductiveAssets`,
`PaymentsToAcquireOilAndGasPropertyAndEquipment`,
`PaymentsToExploreAndDevelopOilAndGasProperties`, `PaymentsToAcquireOilAndGasProperty`,
`PaymentsToAcquireOtherPropertyPlantAndEquipment`.

Mapping rule:
1. If the firm reports a total tag covering all its capex lines (usually
   `PaymentsToAcquireProductiveAssets`), use that total alone.
2. Otherwise, sum the distinct candidate tags the firm reports for the same period.
3. Sanity check: capex must not exceed the firm's total investing outflows. Firms that
   fail are flagged and reviewed by hand against the filing.
4. **Blindness safeguard:** the tag map (`data/tag_map.csv`, one row per firm with tags
   and reasons) is finalized and committed **before** any bust labels are computed.

Finance leases (important for Amazon, for example) are **not** capex here. Where tagged,
they are reported as a separate descriptive line.

### 2.4 Shortfall (the cash-flow gap)

**Plain:** over the last four quarters, the firm spent more on capex than its business
generated in operating cash.

```python
ttm_capex[q] = capex[q-3] + capex[q-2] + capex[q-1] + capex[q]   # all four must exist
ttm_ocf[q]   = ocf[q-3]   + ocf[q-2]   + ocf[q-1]   + ocf[q]
shortfall[q] = ttm_capex[q] > ttm_ocf[q]                         # missing if either is missing
```

(Capex is taken as a positive number. If TTM OCF is negative, any positive capex counts
as a shortfall.)

### 2.5 Lenders step back (primary proxy)

**Plain:** more US banks say they are tightening loan standards for large and
middle-market companies than say they are easing them.

- **Series:** FRED `DRTSCILM`, the Federal Reserve Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey:
  net percentage of domestic banks tightening standards for C&I loans to large and
  middle-market firms. Quarterly. <https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DRTSCILM>
- **Why this proxy.** It is a direct answer from lenders about whether they are pulling
  back. A credit spread is a market price that mixes lender pullback with fear of
  default. The survey is also quarterly, like the filings.
- **Threshold:** `> 0`. That is the natural line where tightening outweighs easing. It is
  not tuned.
- **Timing:** the survey dated the first day of quarter `Q+1` asks about the previous
  three months, so it describes quarter `Q`.

```python
lender_step_back[q] = DRTSCILM[first_day_of(q + 1)] > 0
```

- **Known weakness, stated up front:** this is one economy-wide number, the same for
  every firm in a given quarter. It is not specific to energy lenders, and it says
  nothing about any individual firm's banks.

**Side check (pre-registered, reported next to the primary, never swapped in for it):**
FRED `BAA10Y` (Moody's Baa corporate yield minus 10-year Treasury). The step-back flag is
on when the quarterly average is above its own trailing 20-quarter median.

### 2.6 Signal

```python
signal[q] = shortfall[q] and lender_step_back[q]
```

### 2.7 Bust (two tiers, both fixed now, both reported)

- **B1, primary: bankruptcy.** The company (its own CIK) files an 8-K listing
  **Item 1.03, "Bankruptcy or Receivership."** Source: EDGAR submissions API
  (`https://data.sec.gov/submissions/CIK##########.json`), with the 8-K accession number
  kept. Bust quarter = the calendar quarter of the event date (`reportDate`), or of the
  filing date if no event date is given.
- **B2, secondary: bankruptcy OR a capex collapse.** B1, or the first quarter where
  TTM capex fell to half or less of its highest TTM value in the previous 12 quarters:

```python
capex_cut[q] = ttm_capex[q] <= 0.5 * max(ttm_capex[q-12 : q-1])  # needs >= 4 prior TTM values
```

**Why two tiers.** In 2014–16 oil fell by roughly 70%, and I expect many firms cut capex
by half whether or not they were in trouble. If B2 were the only definition, almost every
firm might count as a bust, and the test could not tell anything apart. That expectation
comes from general knowledge, not from this project's data (see §5).

## 3. Universes (chosen mechanically, never by name)

### Stage 1: descriptive only, no test
Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta, Oracle, roughly 2015 through the latest filed quarter.
These five are the largest US cloud/AI infrastructure spenders named in the original
brief. They have **not** busted, so Stage 1 only describes; it predicts nothing.

### Stage 2: historical test, US oil and gas producers
1. Every filer whose EDGAR SIC code is **1311** (Crude Petroleum & Natural Gas).
2. That reported XBRL `Assets` **≥ US$500 million** in the calendar-year-end-2013 frame
   (`https://data.sec.gov/api/xbrl/frames/us-gaap/Assets/USD/CY2013Q4I.json`). That is
   **before** the oil crash that began in mid-2014, so selection can't use the outcome.
   The frames data includes firms that later went bankrupt or were acquired, which
   protects against survivorship bias.
3. That files quarterly US-GAAP cash-flow data (10-Q/10-K). Foreign filers (20-F/40-F)
   drop out mechanically. Every excluded firm is listed with its reason.
4. Needs **≥ 4 valid TTM quarters in 2013Q1–2016Q4**. Otherwise the firm is excluded
   and listed as "insufficient data."

Honest label: this is "US-listed oil and gas producers," not strictly "shale." SIC 1311
also covers conventional and offshore producers. SIC is the firm's *current* code on
EDGAR, so firms that changed codes may be misplaced.

Data period: 2012Q1 (needed to build the first TTM values) through **2019Q4**, which
gives three years of follow-up after the last possible signal.

### "Booms that ended well" comparison group
Deferred to Stage 2. Its selection rule will be added to this file as a dated addendum
and committed **before** its data is pulled. Firms in the main universe that did not
bust already serve as within-sample examples of booms that didn't end in a bust.

## 4. The test (Stage 2)

Each firm gets exactly one label:

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **TP**, true positive | Busted, and had a signal in at least one of the 12 quarters **strictly before** the bust quarter. |
| **FN**, false negative (miss) | Busted, with **no** signal in the 12 quarters before the bust. Cash flow covered spending, or lenders hadn't stepped back. |
| **FP**, false positive | Did **not** bust from 2013Q1 to 2019Q4, yet had a signal in 2013Q1–2016Q4. The boom survived a shortfall after lenders stepped back. |
| **TN**, true negative | No bust and no signal in 2013Q1–2016Q4. |

**Firms that exited.** A firm that stopped filing without an Item 1.03 8-K (for example,
because it was acquired) is labeled on the data it has and counts as no bust in the
primary table. A sensitivity table drops these firms. They are listed by name.

### Always reported
- The 2×2 confusion table (signal yes/no × bust yes/no), separately for B1 and B2, and
  for primary vs side-check lender proxy and first-reported vs latest-filed values.
- **Base rate:** the bust rate among firms with no signal = FN / (FN + TN).
- Bust rate among signaled firms = TP / (TP + FP), and its difference from the base rate.
- The number of false positives and every FP firm by name.
- Fisher's exact test (two-sided) p-value, labeled as descriptive given the small sample.
- Sample size, stated plainly in every summary.

### How the result is read (fixed now)
1. **Strict reading, the rule as a law.** The brief's failure conditions: the rule
   **fails** if any bust was preceded by cash flow covering spending (any FN caused by
   no shortfall), or if any firm survived a signal (any FP). Under this reading, a single
   counterexample falsifies the rule, and every counterexample is reported.
2. **Probabilistic reading, "usually."** For B1:
   - **Supported (pilot):** bust rate among signaled firms > base rate **and** Fisher p < 0.05.
   - **Mixed:** bust rate among signaled firms > base rate, but p ≥ 0.05.
   - **Not supported:** bust rate among signaled firms ≤ base rate.

Neither reading turns a pilot this size into proof.

## 5. Honesty notes, written before seeing data

- **Prior knowledge.** I already know, in broad strokes, that oil prices crashed in
  2014–16 and that many US producers went bankrupt. Pre-registration protects against
  tuning definitions to *this dataset*. It cannot erase what I knew going in. That is
  why the thresholds are natural lines (`> 0`, capex > OCF, half) rather than tuned
  numbers.
- **Confounding.** The oil price collapse cut operating cash flow (creating shortfalls)
  and caused busts at the same time. With an economy-wide lender proxy, this test cannot
  separate "shortfall plus lenders stepping back caused the bust" from "a commodity crash
  caused everything." This limit will be stated with the results.
- **Firm vs boom.** The rule speaks about booms (whole industries). The test counts
  individual firms. That is a simplification.
- **Missing data stops the work.** If a source fails or a needed number is missing in a
  way the rules above don't cover, the work stops and the author decides. Nothing is
  invented or estimated.

## 6. Addendum A1 (2026-11-12): comparison boom, pre-registered before its data is pulled

**Why the rule picks the comparison by size, not by outcome.** Choosing a sector
*because* its boom "ended well" would be selecting on the outcome, the hindsight
section 3 forbids. Instead, the comparison group is **the largest non-energy building
boom of the same years**, picked by a capex-growth rule. Whether it ended well is then
*measured* with the same bust definitions, not assumed. At the time of writing, I don't
know which sector the rule will pick.

**Selection rule (mechanical):**
1. Candidate firms: every filer with XBRL `Assets` ≥ US$500M in the CY2013Q4I frame
   (same frame and threshold as section 3).
2. Each firm's capex for calendar 2011 and 2014 comes from the XBRL frames API
   (`frames/us-gaap/<tag>/USD/CY2011.json` and `CY2014.json`):
   `PaymentsToAcquirePropertyPlantAndEquipment`, or `PaymentsToAcquireProductiveAssets`
   if the first is absent for that firm and year. A firm needs a value in both years.
3. SIC = the firm's current SIC code from the EDGAR submissions API.
4. Eligible sectors: 4-digit SIC codes with at least **15** such firms, **excluding**
   energy-linked codes so the comparison isn't hit by the same oil shock: SIC 1000–1499
   (mining, incl. oil and gas), 2911 (refining), 3533 (oil-field machinery), 4610–4619
   (pipelines), 4922–4924 (natural gas transmission and distribution), 5171–5172
   (petroleum wholesale), 6792 (oil royalty traders).
5. Boom size = (sum of 2014 capex) ÷ (sum of 2011 capex) across that sector's eligible
   firms. **The sector with the largest ratio is the comparison boom.**
6. The comparison universe is then built exactly like section 3: EDGAR's company list
   for that SIC × `Assets` ≥ $500M in CY2013Q4I.

**Analysis:** identical to sections 2 and 4: same definitions, thresholds and windows,
both versions (A and B, CHANGELOG v1.1), and all variants. Reported:
- the comparison group's own confusion table, bust rate and signal rate;
- the share of **non-bankrupt** firms that showed the signal (the false-alarm rate), side
  by side with the oil and gas group;
- a pooled table (both groups together) as a secondary result.

**How it's read (fixed now):** if the signal fires for most firms in a boom with few or
no bankruptcies, that is further evidence the signal is not specific to busts. If the
signal rarely fires there, the comparison does not count against the rule. Neither
outcome rescues or condemns the rule alone: it is one more boom.
